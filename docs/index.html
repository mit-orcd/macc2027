What this is

Project MACC 2027 is a multi-month contest that awards prizes for the best uses of agentic coding/programming/software-development to advance research and education missions at MIT. It runs across the Fall 2026 term and finishes during IAP in January 2027. It is not a weekend hackathon: a goal is to understand and explore how agentic coding/programming/software-development tools can produce outcomes that might lead to positive sustained capabilities or worthwhile results for a project, a lab or a class - and for the people in them - even after the contest ends.

Inspiration for the contest has a seventy-plus year long thread. In 1957, MIT's Computation Center published CODING for the IBM 704 and taught a generation to program early foundations of today's world on a room-sized, shared machine. In 2027, a student can run capable open-weight coding agents on a desktop machine they own - and in the cloud they can run closed-weight models on million-dollar collections of GPUs and other technologies. The contest asks what MIT researchers and educators might do with these new capabilities. The name is a shout-out to the earlier MIT Project MAC, which, starting nearly 70 years ago, seeded similar questions about possible futures for computing in research and education.

What can the MIT community do for MIT research and education in the open-source software programming, engineering, and development space when they are relatively unconstrained - free to use whatever AI tools they feel are appropriate - and to use them thoughtfully - in order to have a positive impact on research or education?

How the contest is organized

Entries declare where they want to compete along two axes.

Areas

Seven areas aligned to MIT's own structure: the five schools (Science; Engineering; Architecture and Planning; Humanities, Arts and Social Sciences; Management), the Schwarzman College of Computing, and MIT Education (residential teaching and Open Learning). Each area has a $2,000 Area Prize, and one $5,000 Grand Prize is awarded across all areas - details on the prizes page.

Technology stance

Open-weight models, open-source harnesses, commercial models and agents, bring-your-own scaffolds, robust research software engineering, or deterministic, verifiable modeling. Entrants pick and mash up whatever makes sense.

The rules in brief

  • Prize finalists are judged on three things together: an open-source piece of software the team created, a short write-up - including team learnings around ways in which humans and agents worked together - and a live presentation by finalist teams at the January showcase (demo and Q&A). Finalists must open-source their work.1
    • We want to learn how communities might share and evolve AI generated software and tooling advances together.
  • Teams choose where they are judged. Entries select areas and technology stances for judging. Judging will assess accordingly; one entry can contend in several categories. Teams must include at least one member of the MIT community, students or employees - undergraduate students, graduate students, postdocs, researchers, faculty and PIs, and administration are all invited.
  • Three headline criteria: contribution or credible potential for contribution to the elected research or education area (including impact on human insight, understanding and judgement); technical capability, innovation, and originality; and robustness and reproducibility of the outcome (ways others can repeat, build-on, examine and learn from your work).
Overall, judging will explore how a projects learning can enhance human and machine knowledge and capabilities and boost the creative, thoughtful process of research/education discovery and paced reflective iteration.

What we provide - and what entrants may bring

MIT Office of Research Computing and Data (ORCD) operates two reference setups on its own hardware, run by ORCD behind gateways, with sandboxed, pinned execution so results can be re-run and checked. They are not a ceiling. Contestants may use any hardware they like: their own rigs, lab clusters, cloud, or alternative silicon. Entries built elsewhere will still submit an open-source end product for judging. No prize requires using the reference setups, and vendor-supported or home-grown approaches are welcome.
